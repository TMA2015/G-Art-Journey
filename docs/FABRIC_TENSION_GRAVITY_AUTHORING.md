# Draw Fabric from Tension & Gravity — production specification

Status: **final poster owner-approved; release candidate wired in PR #36**

This is the fourth Phase 2A Core Drawing Skills item. It should teach why folds appear before teaching decorative wrinkle patterns.

## 1. Learning outcome

A beginner should learn to ask **what is holding, pulling, bending or compressing the cloth** before drawing lines.

The poster should teach:
1. support / tension points
2. gravity and pull direction
3. stretch versus compression
4. large fold groups before small wrinkles
5. thickness, overlap and the form underneath

This is a cause-and-effect lesson, not a catalogue of every named fold type.

## 2. Visual identity

- portrait infographic, roughly 3:4
- warm off-white paper / sketchbook background
- graphite / pencil artwork
- pastel accents only in headings, arrows, step numbers and small callouts
- purple **G-Art Journey** logo in a quiet upper corner
- English-only public copy using short common words
- no copied garment, pose, fold chart or tutorial composition

The body is only a support for the fabric lesson. Do not make the face or character design the visual focus.

## 3. Approved layout direction

Use the panel-first workflow. Lock the page grid and teaching job of each panel before rendering detailed art.

### A. Hero study — Read one sleeve

Use one large bent sleeve on a simplified arm/torso.

Show four causes clearly:
- shoulder / seam as a support point
- gravity pulling the loose fabric down
- stretch on the outside of the bend
- compression and shorter folds on the inside of the elbow

Keep the hand outside the crop unless it is needed. The elbow, upper arm and forearm must remain anatomically believable.

Main label:
**Find what holds and pulls the cloth**

Small callouts:
- **support**
- **gravity**
- **stretch**
- **compression**

### B. Four cause cards

These are separate examples, not a cumulative sequence.

#### 1. One support point
A hanging cloth or loose sleeve held from one area.

Teach:
- folds start from the support
- gravity pulls down
- long fold groups are more useful than many tiny lines

Label:
**One support: folds drop down**

#### 2. Two tension points
A cloth stretched between two anchors, like a small drape.

Teach:
- the fabric pulls toward both anchors
- the middle can sag
- fold direction should explain the pull

Label:
**Two supports: cloth pulls between them**

#### 3. Compression at a bend
A bent sleeve or trouser knee.

Teach:
- fabric bunches where two body forms move closer
- the opposite side stretches more
- compression creates shorter, tighter fold groups

Label:
**At a bend: one side bunches**

#### 4. Wrap and overlap
A sleeve or skirt wrapping around a simple cylinder/leg.

Teach:
- cloth follows the 3D form underneath
- overlapping edges create front/back order
- folds should turn with the form, not sit flat on top

Label:
**Wrap the form, then overlap**

## 4. Reference mini-grid

Use six small studies to make the principle practical without crowding the poster:

1. hanging skirt
2. loose sleeve
3. bent elbow
4. seated knee
5. moving scarf
6. light cloth vs. heavier cloth

These are **examples**, not six universal fold formulas.

Keep the drawings simple enough that the cause remains readable.

## 5. Key tips

Use four short reminders:
1. **Find the support first.**
2. **Follow gravity and pull.**
3. **Draw big folds before wrinkles.**
4. **Let cloth wrap the form.**

Optional footer:
**If every wrinkle is equally important, the big fold pattern is probably missing.**

## 6. Mandatory fabric QA

Before publication:
- every fold can be traced back to a believable support, pull, bend, compression area or movement
- gravity direction is consistent within each study
- stretched areas and compressed areas do not contradict the pose
- large fold groups remain visible before small wrinkles
- the underlying arm, leg or torso volume remains believable
- folds wrap around cylindrical forms where appropriate
- overlaps show clear front/back order
- cloth does not look pasted onto the body
- no random zigzags or repeated parallel wrinkles without a cause
- line weight and value do not make every fold equally strong
- material examples remain examples; do not claim one fold pattern fits every fabric
- hands are omitted unless needed; any visible whole hand must pass the five-digit QA rule
- text remains readable on mobile
- G-Art Journey logo is present and uses the purple site identity

Inspect each high-risk panel at full resolution **before** assembling the final infographic, then inspect the composite again.

## 7. What to avoid

Reject or revise a panel if:
- folds are added only to fill empty space
- the fold direction ignores gravity or the tension points
- compression appears on the stretched side of a bend
- the same wrinkle pattern is copied across unrelated garments
- every wrinkle is outlined with the same strength
- body anatomy underneath becomes impossible
- a detailed garment hides the teaching cause
- colored rendering overwhelms the graphite-first lesson
- the poster becomes a dense taxonomy of fold names

## 8. Academic grounding

Use these principles as authoring references, not as copy to reproduce:

### Proko — Intro to Drapery
https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/intro-to-drapery

Useful authoring principles:
- landmarks, seams and garment edges can act as tension/support points
- gravity pulls down from those points
- simplify folds into larger shapes and gestures
- fabric must settle around the 3D form underneath
- overlap helps show depth

### Proko — Intro to Compression / Half-Lock Folds
https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/intro-to-compression-half-lock-folds

Useful authoring principle:
- when two body areas move closer at a bend, fabric compresses and forms shorter grouped folds

### Art Prof — How to Draw Clothing
https://artprof.org/learn/how-to-draw-clothing/

Useful authoring principle:
- fold families can help observation, but beginners should first look for the largest shapes and curves rather than copy every wrinkle

Public wording must remain original G-Art language.

## 9. Panel-first production workflow

For this poster, do not ask one dense generation to solve the whole page at once.

1. Lock the infographic grid and exact teaching job of every panel.
2. Produce the hero sleeve separately.
3. Produce each cause card separately.
4. Produce the six small reference studies separately or in small safe groups.
5. Inspect every panel for anatomy, fold cause, direction, overlap and crop.
6. Repair or reject a panel before compositing.
7. Assemble only approved panels into the final G-Art layout.
8. Add the purple G-Art Journey logo and concise English labels.
9. Perform a final whole-page mobile/readability/continuity QA pass.
10. Optimize and pin the approved source, then wire the guide/test/build release.

This workflow is intentionally stricter than a single-shot infographic generation because small local errors are easier to detect and replace before composition.
